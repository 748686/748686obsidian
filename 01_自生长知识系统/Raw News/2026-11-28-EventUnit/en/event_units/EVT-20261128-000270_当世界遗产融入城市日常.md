## Event Overview

On November 28, 2026, *People's Daily* published an article titled "When World Heritage Integrates into Urban Daily Life" (当世界遗产融入城市日常), authored by Wang Rui. The article examines the post-inscription challenges and strategies for three recently designated Chinese World Heritage sites: the Grand Canal (specifically Yangzhou), Quanzhou: The Ancient Maritime Silk Road Port, and Jingdezhen Ceramics Kiln Sites. The core argument is that inscription is not a terminus but a starting point; the long-term value of these sites depends on how post-inscription archaeology, narrative frameworks, and spatial practices integrate heritage conservation with local urban development and daily life. The article highlights divergent trajectories: Yangzhou focuses on expanding urban archaeology to enrich the canal's heritage narrative, Quanzhou is dealing with the tension between tourism industrialization and the original urban fabric, and Jingdezhen is in the early stage of utilizing "living heritage" infrastructure (such as kiln-ruin walls and sewage systems) in its modern urban context.

## Core Facts

*   **Timeline of Inscriptions (2014–2026):** Between 2014 and 2026, three sites were added to the World Heritage List: the Grand Canal (China), Quanzhou: The Ancient Maritime Silk Road Port, and Jingdezhen Ceramics Kiln Sites.
*   **Yangzhou (Grand Canal):**
    *   Archaeological work by the Institute of Archaeology, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, at the Yangzhou City Site has been ongoing since 1987.
    *   A five-year work plan has been established for continued urban archaeology.
    *   The Yangzhou China Grand Canal Museum, built post-inscription, extends its display scope to cities along the canal.
    *   12 years have passed since the Grand Canal's inscription.
    *   Current challenge: The commercial formats in streets like Dongguan Street are becoming indistinguishable from similar tourism districts nationwide, threatening the site's uniqueness.
*   **Quanzhou:**
    *   Archaeological excavations at the Nan Wai Zongzheng Si (South Foreign Zongzheng Si) and Shi Bo Si (Maritime Trade Office) sites supported the nomination narrative.
    *   The Nan Wai Zongzheng Si Archaeological Site Park officially opened in July 2026.
    *   Protection and display projects for the Shi Bo Si are still in progress.
    *   Excavations at the Anxi Qingyang Xiaocaoppu Iron Smelting Site continue.
    *   5 years have passed since Quanzhou's inscription.
    *   Current state: Described by researchers as "half the city wears flowers, half the city has duck" (半城簪花半城鸭), indicating that hair flower crowns (*zan hua*) and ginger mother duck (*jiang mu ya*) have become dominant commercial formats, altering the living spaces of original residents and traditional industry ecosystems.
*   **Jingdezhen:**
    *   Joint archaeological work began in 2024, expanding the scope to include urban infrastructure: city walls, roads, shrines, wharves, and sewers.
    *   The city's urban fabric was shaped by ceramics production (kilns built along rivers, markets forming around kilns).
    *   Qing Dynasty sewage systems constructed from waste porcelain and bricks still function as main drainage channels.
    *   Alley walls built from waste kiln bricks are widely preserved.
    *   These are classified as "living heritage" (active infrastructure) rather than merely display relics.
    *   Cultural tourism industrialization has already launched premium tour routes based on 45 heritage points.
*   **General Principle:** Post-inscription archaeology must support protection planning and display; protection practice provides new directions for archaeology. The goal is to ensure heritage contributes to long-term local development through integration into daily urban life.

## Event Conclusion

The event highlights the critical "post-inscription phase" of World Heritage management in China. For Yangzhou, Quanzhou, and Jingdezhen, the challenge is no longer just preservation of physical relics, but managing the dynamic integration of heritage into the living urban fabric. Yangzhou's strategy leans toward academic expansion and museum mediation, Quanzhou faces pressure from commercial homogenization that impacts resident life, and Jingdezhen must innovate by managing "living infrastructure" derived from its industrial history. The central insight is that archaeological work must continue to evolve from a support for nomination to a driver of ongoing urban planning and conservation, ensuring that World Heritage status serves local development rather than freezing the city in time.
