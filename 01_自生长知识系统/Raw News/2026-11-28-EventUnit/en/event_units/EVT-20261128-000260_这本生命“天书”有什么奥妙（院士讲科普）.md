## Event Overview
On November 28, 2026, *People's Daily* published an article in its "Academician Science Popularization" column titled "What are the Mysteries of this 'Heavenly Book' of Life" (*这本生命“天书”有什么奥妙*). Authored by Xie Xiaoliang, a member of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) and Director of the Changping Laboratory, the article coincides with the 25th anniversary of the first draft of the Human Genome Project. The piece discusses the history of genomics, the impact of sequencing technology on medicine, and future research paradigms involving artificial intelligence, based on remarks from an international symposium held by the Changping Laboratory.

## Core Facts
*   **Publication Date:** November 28, 2026.
*   **Source Media:** *People's Daily* (Page 06: Science & New Knowledge).
*   **Author:** Xie Xiaoliang (Chinese Academy of Sciences Academician; Director of Changping Laboratory).
*   **Occasion:** The 25th anniversary of the publication of the first draft of the human genome sequence (originally published in February 2001).
*   **Related Event:** The Changping Laboratory held an International Symposium on the 25th Anniversary of the Human Genome Sequence Draft, attended by over 100 scientists from various countries and regions.
*   **Key Scientific Data Points Cited:**
    *   The Human Genome Project (HGP) started in 1990.
    *   In Feb 2001, the International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium and Celera Genomics published the "Working Draft of the Human Genome" in *Nature* and *Science*, respectively.
    *   Humans have approximately 22,000 genes (far fewer than previously speculated).
    *   Coding regions account for only 2% of the genome; the 98% non-coding region determines traits like height, skin color, and susceptibility to chronic diseases.
    *   Chinese scientists (led by Yang Huanming and others) sequenced ~1% of the human genome (approx. 30 million base pairs on Chromosome 3) in the 1990s.
    *   In 2001, fewer than 900 genes were linked to human hereditary diseases; by 2026, over 7,500 genetic disease phenotypes have identified molecular bases in the International Human Mendelian Genetics Database.
    *   Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), pioneered by Luke Kwok-Yan Leung (CUIHK) in 1997, has been used over 100 million times globally.
    *   In 2014, the first healthy baby born via MALBAC (Multiple-Displacement Amplification) preimplantation genetic testing was delivered at Peking University Third Hospital.
    *   Xie Xiaoliang's team developed "DNA Fluorescence Generation Sequencing" in 2011.
    *   Peking University's Huang Yanyi team's LoopCell technology reduces sequencing costs to hundreds of RMB.
    *   On November 24, 2026, a collaborative team led by Changping Laboratory/Peking University (with Peking University Third Hospital and PLA General Hospital) achieved simultaneous assessment of single-gene and multi-gene risks via non-invasive embryo culture fluid, with near-100% diagnostic accuracy for reportable single-gene diseases.

*   **Cancer Treatment:** Shift towards precision medicine based on molecular characteristics rather than just tumor location.
*   **Reproductive Health:** Widespread use of NIPT (>100 million times) and advanced preimplantation genetic testing to prevent single-gene diseases.
*   **Cost Reduction:** Domestic Chinese technologies have reduced sequencing costs to hundreds of RMB, making it more accessible than US-based options according to the source.

## Event Conclusion
This event marks the 25th anniversary of the human genome draft, commemorated by a specialized science popularization column in *People's Daily* featuring Xie Xiaoliang. The narrative serves a dual purpose: reflecting on the historical significance of the HGP and highlighting recent Chinese scientific advancements in genomic medicine, including cost-effective sequencing, non-invasive diagnostics, and AI-driven research. It positions China as a key contributor to global genomics, emphasizing both national achievements and international collaboration. The article underscores that while the "book" of life is readable, its complexity (98% non-coding regions) continues to present new scientific and ethical challenges.
