# Event Name
The Three Imperial Edicts (Zhaoshu) from the Xuanquanzhi Wall Inscriptions

## Event Overview
This event centers on the historical and archaeological analysis of three specific imperial edicts discovered on the wall fragments (referred to as "wall books" or "bishu") of the Xuanquanzhi site, an ancient relay station and administrative post in the Dunhuang region. Excavated between 1990 and 1992, the site yielded over 35,000 bamboo and wooden slips, of which more than 23,000 contain writing. The analysis of wall inscriptions demonstrates that, alongside standard bamboo/wooden slips and stone steles, a third distinct form of Han-dynasty documentation existed: "public notice documents" (gongshi wenshu) written directly onto the walls of official relay buildings. These edicts were placed in "visible locations" where officials, messengers, and travelers could easily read them. The identified edicts span from the reign of Emperor Cheng of Han to Emperor Guangwu of the Later Han, providing concrete evidence of central government administrative procedures, agricultural regulations, and public health policies in the frontier regions.

## Core Facts
*   **Site and Excavation:** The Xuanquanzhi site was excavated from 1990 to 1992. It yielded over 35,000 slips, with over 23,000 containing text. Wall fragments with inscriptions ("bishu") were found in the collapsed gray soil of the structures.
*   **Nature of Documents:** The wall inscriptions represent a third form of Han-dynasty document preservation, distinct from slips and stone steles. They functioned as public notices for passersby.
*   **Edict 1 (Emperor Cheng of Han, 32 BCE):**
    *   **Context:** Issued in the second month of the first year of the Jianshi era (32 BCE) following a comet appearing in the east and a fire in an ancestral temple.
    *   **Content:** Announced a new policy direction of "broad tolerance and harmony," a general amnesty, and rewards for the imperial clan, officials, and rural residents.
    *   **Significance:** The text matches the *Book of Han* (Hanshu). It details the administrative chain of command for distributing edicts: from the Imperial Counselor to the Chancellor, then to generals, prefects, and finally to the Dunhuang border. It specifies reward distribution (e.g., silk for "Sanlao" elders and model filial/humane/working citizens).
*   **Edict 2 (Emperor Ping of Han / Wang Mang era, 5 CE):**
    *   **Title:** "Four Seasons and Monthly Ordinal Edict" (Sishi Yue Ling Zhao Tiao) from the fifth year of the Yuanshi era (5 CE).
    *   **Context:** Issued during the regency of Empress Dowager Wang Zhengjun and the reform program of Wang Mang.
    *   **Content:** 50 articles functioning as an agricultural calendar and code of conduct.
    *   **Specifics:** Prohibits tree-felling in the first spring month (allowed in the eighth month when leaves fall); prohibits draining rivers/ponds for fishing or burning forests in the second spring month to protect breeding wildlife.
    *   **Significance:** Aligns with ancient ecological principles found in the *Book of Rites* and is noted for its relevance to modern sustainability concepts. This artifact is a famous exhibit at the Gansu Slip Museum.
*   **Edict 3 (Emperor Guangwu of Later Han, 25–56 CE):**
    *   **Content:** A medical edict listing prescriptions for various ailments, including stroke, diarrhea, colds, headaches, gastric bi, sore throat, and heart pain.
    *   **Details:** Mentions specific drugs (licorice, warm wine) and administration methods. The text is reconstructed from multiple joined wall fragments.
    *   **Administration:** Commands local officials to procure drugs ("shiyao") and distribute them to the populace.
    *   **Significance:** Acts as a "public health bulletin" on the Silk Road. Archaeological finds of Han-dynasty paper used to wrap medicine corroborate the logistical aspect of this edict.

## Event Conclusion
The analysis of the Xuanquanzhi wall inscriptions reveals a distinct form of Han-dynasty public administration where imperial edicts were posted on the walls of relay stations for immediate public visibility. The three identified edicts—from Emperor Cheng of Han (32 BCE) to Emperor Guangwu (25–56 CE)—demonstrate the extension of central government control over frontier regions, covering legal amnesty, ecological agriculture, and public health. These artifacts serve as a critical bridge between the central imperial court and the frontier populace, offering a concrete glimpse into the daily administrative and social life of the Silk Road era.
