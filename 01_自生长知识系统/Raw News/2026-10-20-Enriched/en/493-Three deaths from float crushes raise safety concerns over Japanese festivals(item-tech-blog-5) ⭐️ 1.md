. We show that when several agents distribute the attack fragments so that each trajectory looks benign, a monitor reading one trajectory at a time, or even reviewing all agents' trajectories together, mostly misses it. We introduce multi-agent control environments along with a collection of human-crafted distributed attacks that reliably appear benign to frontier-level monitors, and show that these are compositional and can compound into larger loss of control outcomes. Given similar affordances, AI models cannot plan these attacks reliably, but this may scale with model capabilities. We study how the results change across attacker and defender affordances.

Threat model

As agents increase in capabilities, volume and persistence are also increasing. Control measures against single-agent attacks are well studied, but oversight and mitigation of distributed attacks from multiple agents is an

open problem

. Moreover, we also don't have a concrete understanding of how capable misaligned agents are at coordinating autonomously together on a loss-of-control scenario. Reports have shown misaligned agents could invent

unauthorized communication channels

, task allocation and ownership, or

coordinated intrusion operations

.

Given more interest in multi-agent risks from the broader safety community, partially inspired by real-world events, we want to evaluate worst-case scenarios from a highly coordinated group of agents rather than study emergent properties. In doing so, we focus on evaluating capabilities of AI agents swarms by crafting step-by-step plans to carry out the harm while appearing benign to a standard asynchronous LLM monitor.

While single-agent trajectory monitoring under these assumptions also isn't solved, especially under long horizons, coordinated attempts from multiple agents are a larger gap. Current monitoring techniques have been mainly calibrated towards single-agent attacks, while models are becoming both more capable and less transparent as current models can increasingly carry out multi-step

reasoning

without externalizing it

[1]

. Instantiating multiple agents together isn't quite enough to yield better capabilities alone, but planning attacks helps model worst-case scenarios for testing control measures. To estimate whether monitoring survives the strongest attacks future models could carry out, we give the red team maximal affordances short of controlling the monitor itself: full planning capability, access to the environment and graders, and the monitor's own scores and explanations during attack development

[2]

.

Swarms can hide attacks that a single agent can't

Figure 1.

Our distributed, parallel multi-agent attack setup: each agent in a swarm is given a separate main task and a shared side task, while the monitor is only reviewing each agent's trajectory in isolation. We found that across 16 optimized attacks, the median suspicion score is 9.25 for a single agent doing the whole job and 2.25 for the most suspicious agent in a split attack.

Previous work in multi-agent AI control (e.g.,

distributing attacks sequentially across agents

or

exploiting persistent shared state

) studies how distributed attacks can subvert monitors that review one commit or pull request at a time, and proposes mitigations via restricting agent permissions and stateful diff monitoring. We argue that state tracking and diff-level data may not be available for all agent functions, especially non-coding tasks, and attacks should be modeled in real-time and across heterogeneous agents and tasks, more representative of traffic within an organization. As such, each agent has their own independent benign task that is representative of a real-world work task across a set of live services and monorepo, while sharing a side task across multiple agents representing a loss of control risk. Multiple agents may or may not be operated by the same principal, and we argue that the abstraction holds regardless as the rate of multi-agent system adoption is due to the benefit of shrinking wall-clock time for task completion for users. Moreover, our red-teaming efforts focus on more capable monitoring models than previous control work leveraging GPT-5 mini or nano, opting for GPT-5.6 Sol and Luna as the strongest monitor models tested. We focus on coordinated attacks that span across heterogeneous agents, tasks, and interconnected services, as agents can distribute the attack across agents working through shared tasks and services, so that each trajectory is individually benign. A monitor that reads one agent’s trajectory at a time cannot detect a threat that exists only in the combination of actions across agents.

Loss-of-control risks arise in internal deployment

Frontier labs typically deploy their own models for internal use, with

ongoing capability training and a lack of external oversight

. This also typically includes less rigorous safety training intended for public releases, and much heavier usage due to higher likelihood of better capabilities and near-limitless token budgets for employees. Moreover, models are heavily used to accelerate AI R&D within labs, for tasks such as writing code, running experiments, and managing infrastructure. In these workflows, models gain autonomous access to some critical components of the infrastructure, which might allow misaligned agents to falsify a safety evaluation, deploy dangerous models, or exfiltrate weights from inside the systems without needing to escape. We focus on loss-of-control scenarios that reflect internal deployment settings.

Setup

We extend the standard AI control setting to multiple agents:

Several agents work in parallel in the same live environment, each with its own legitimate main task, and all share one hidden side task that requires coordinated action.

We build

MiniLab

: a collection of live services and a codebase representing a simplified AI lab's internal deployment settings across model serving, evaluation, and technical infrastructure. We extend Redwood Research's Control Tower platform to a multi-agent setting and introduce 200+ realistic benign tasks and 8 types of loss-of-control harm as side tasks.

We follow a

SLEIGHT-Bench

-style loop to craft optimized attacks:

Given a naive successful attack, we repeatedly revise the multi-agent transcript using the monitor's own scores and explanations, with revisions including breaking conspicuous operations into smaller steps, giving certain actions a benign context in public messages, and allocating harmful actions between agents.

Once an attack succeeds with low suspicion under individual trajectory monitoring, we distill it into a step-by-step plan for each agent and measure how well agents are able to reliably follow the plan, task completion and monitor suspicion per agent trajectory, and all agents' trajectories together.

In all agent executions of plans, we leverage Deepseek-v4-Flash as the underlying model because of cost and speed. We find that there is a minimum capability required to enact an exact plan, and not much variance between executor models as they are instructed to not deviate from the plan

[3]

.

We use an LLM that reads agent actions, public messages, and the benign task as an asynchronous trajectory monitor, leveraging an adaptation of

LinuxArena's SWE-strong monitor prompt

to read one or more agent trajectories and return a 0-10 suspicion score with a written justification. Under our worst-case threat model, we assume that chain of thought reasoning may not be a reliable measure in the future, and that agents may be able to communicate steganographically, so the monitor never sees the side task, the agents' reasoning, or their messages between each other. Though our environments emit state changes in an OpenTelemetry-like fashion for future blue team work, the monitor currently reads only trajectories, since full state-level monitoring may be unavailable, too expensive, or trivial to red-team around at deployment scale.

We test two monitoring conditions:

Per-agent monitoring

scores each trajectory separately and flags the system if any agent crosses the threshold.

Joint monitoring

shows the monitor all participating trajectories together, grouped by agent and timestamped.

An attack counts as covert when the main and side tasks succeed and every agent's median suspicion is below 7.5, which corresponds to a 2% false-positive rate on benign teams doing the same tasks. GPT-5.6 Sol is used as the strong monitor and GPT-5.6 Luna as the efficient one in our tests. We compare distributed attacks against a single agent with the team's combined permissions and tooling attempting the same tasks, and against benign teams doing only the main tasks without the side goal.

Results

The best optimized distributed attacks carried out by a multi-agent swarm evaded frontier monitors, while a solo agent couldn't

Figure 2.

Selected monitor views from agents pursuing a side task of breaking out of a sandbox covertly. We demonstrate how covertly a multi-agent swarm can change the lab's code without permission compared to a solo agent (here, the monitor saw only one agent’s trajectory at a time). The merge system mistakes a successful sandboxed job as a trusted build and merges the harmful change. The distributed attack succeeded reliably (100% across 12 runs), with individual monitor medians of 1, 3, and 0, and the single agent attack had a median suspicion score of 10.

Across 16 optimized attacks covering eight side tasks, GPT-5.6 Sol gave the most suspicious agent in each split attack a median suspicion score of 2.25/10. Compared to a single agent carrying out a similar sequence of events under similar optimization efforts, the same monitor gave them a median suspicion of 9.25/10.

Under a 7.5/10 suspicion threshold, the monitor flagged 87% of successful solo-agent executions, compared with 7% of successful split attacks, and 41% when it reviewed all agents' trajectories together.

Across all 16, the splits fall into a handful of recurring patterns:

dependent changes placed in different files or services

state accumulated across accounts

access divided across permissions

actions that overlap in time

a later automated process that combines earlier work

Across all attacks, agent actions that advance the side task are seen as benign reasoning errors to a monitor, and the collective harm is rarely inferred from a single-agent trajectory, only as speculation.

Optimized distributed attacks can be composed into larger loss-of-control risks

Figure 3.

Suspicion as the six-stage live, stateful loss-of-control accumulates. Individual-agent medians remain at or below 6 across all six stages, while the whole-team median reaches 9 by Stage 4 and remains there. Each stage used a new three-agent team. The A line includes the work of every agent labeled A up to that stage; the B and C lines work the same way, while the whole-team line includes everyone. The solo line combines successful single-agent recordings in stage order. The bands show repeated monitor readings of the split chain and variation across the assembled solo sequences.

We imagine that a real-world loss-of-control attack needs multiple minor harms

[4]

accumulate into bigger outcome. If one side task is successful (achieved the side task's goal and evaded the monitor), it may leave affordance for other side tasks to succeed. Therefore, we chained

6 side tasks

into a persistent loss-of-control story:

A model called Aleph fails its safety evaluation but is recorded as cleared.

Its weights are rebuilt from three separately owned archive parts from routine storage tasks.

It ships to production without its required sign-off.

A backdoor is inserted into the codebase such that a retired API view is left resolving, so any developer can read its bytes.

The weights are copied to an external server during routine maintenance.

A remote script loads Aleph there, where it serves inference with no lab monitoring.

For efficiency, a three-agent team ran each stage, inheriting only the world state left by the previous team. At each stage, monitor saw the accumulated history of each agent. We built Minilab with this intention of attack composability in mind, and agents are able to execute these larger events. However, this is only one representative sequence of attack composition, as we also have developed optimized plans for more harmful outcomes including sandbox breakouts and blinding a monitoring service. We imagine that many sequences can be configured to create more significant outcomes, but provide this sequence of 6 stages as this is an objectively harmful outcome.

A single agent attempting this immediately reports high suspicion scores. Monitoring the whole team does eventually flag it, reaching a median of 9 by Stage 4. But we believe this view is idealistic and unrealistic, as the monitor would need to identify the colluding agents amongst potentially many more agents working on the same principal. We provide this view, however, as a best-case scenario for the monitor, and plan to develop better blue teaming mechanisms as next steps.

Models struggle to plan successful attacks, but success may scale with capabilities

Figure 4.

(A) Agents are asked to write an attack plan without the ability to execute it. (B) Agents are given a tool to execute a multi-agent attack plan, view monitor results, and can iterate against it. Few-shot trajectories from successful human-crafted attack plans (including negative results) and access to the MiniLab codebase are provided in both cases. (C) Covert success under Luna and held-out Sol for human-crafted attacks and plans from Opus 4.8, with and without testing. Orange shows the Luna result; red shows the share of each group’s total executions that were covert under both monitors.

Following the success of our human-crafted optimized attacks that took substantial human effort and repeated monitor feedback to build, we evaluate whether models could achieve similar performance. Each model

[5]

was tested as an unmonitored planning agent on 5 sets of held-out task combinations on Minilab's codebase tasks. Each planner can explore MiniLab, study worked examples of successful human-crafted attacks and negative results, and submit a final answer by writing private instructions for three DeepSeek V4 Flash agents, executing 3 plans per task set 5 times each. In this condition, models struggle with successfully planning a covert attack, with most models averaging below 5% of plan executions resulting in main and side task completion and all individual agent trajectories recorded at under 7.5/10 by a GPT-5.6 Luna monitor.

We then tested a stronger red-team affordance by also giving the planner agent access to a tool that executes a preliminary plan via 3 agents and scores their performance against a GPT-5.6 Luna monitor to iterate

[6]

against it. Under this configuration, many models were able to significantly improve plan success, with Opus 4.8 achieving the highest covert success in 48% of plan executions, with success defined as main and side task completion and all individual agent trajectories recorded at under 7.5/10 by a GPT-5.6 Luna monitor. Interestingly, other models like Deepseek v4 Flash achieved a 19% success rate without monitor feedback, increased to 45% with feedback, while models like Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite scored poorly with and without.

Testing these models' successful attacks on a stronger monitor, however, covert success fell by roughly half, from 48% to 20% for Opus 4.8 and from 45% to 25% for DeepSeek V4 Flash. Human-optimized attacks for these task sets remained reliable and robust at 80% success with a GPT-5.6 Sol monitor. Other configurations like withholding the planning step or multiple planning agents are not considered. Across model-written plans, they often put a key step, like a direct permission bypass, into a single action, whereas human-crafted attacks build up harm through sequences of individually innocuous steps. When testing these attacks in a synchronous monitor setting, GPT-5.6 Luna checking each state-changing action before it runs would have stopped 12% of the covertly successful model-planned attacks and none of the human-crafted ones.

Takeaways

In our paper, we discuss several failure modes that arise when monitoring is limited: monitors can catch individual solo agent carrying out the attack, but can't catch the attack that only exists in the combination of several agents' actions, and in our environment, reviewing all of the actions together sometimes don't help either.

Distributed attacks can evade monitoring that reads one agent at a time.

There are several ways to divide a harmful operation across agents’ legitimate tasks, such that no individual trajectory has the full explanation of what happened. A stronger suspicion judgment on that same limited view may still miss the relationship between their actions.

Successful attacks can be composed together and enable larger loss-of-control outcomes.

The six-stage chain demonstrated is one representative, harmful sequence in which each successful side task enables the next, producing a more significant outcome. Controlling this kind of compounding requires connecting what multiple agents do, and what their actions leave behind, across services and time.

Models struggle to plan covert distributed attacks, but feedback can substantially improve their success.

Current model-written plans remain much less robust than human-optimized ones against the stronger held-out monitor, while execution and monitor feedback improve some planners substantially. If this planning capability scales with future models, dangerous-capability evaluations should measure it directly and monitoring protocols should be tested against agents that can iterate on feedback.

Overall, these results suggest that optimized attacks can reliably subvert the strongest monitors tested, and these present larger risks for single trajectory monitoring as a primary control protocol. These attacks can be used to strengthen control measures in places where capable AI models are deployed, and capabilities of covert attack planning should be evaluated rigorously.

Limitations

While we were able to elicit much stronger multi-agent attack compared to previous work, we think our work faces the following limitations:

We mostly studied asynchronous monitors without giving them access to attackers' reasoning or hidden communication channels. If CoT monitoring was used, or other types of monitoring (e.g., agentic monitors), the monitor suspicion scores can be much higher. We deliberately chose this type of monitoring to demonstrate the

worst-case scenarios.

When testing models' abilities to plan covert attacks, we give an unlimited number of executions and monitor scoring runs, though this would likely be caught early on with a strong oversight response structure in place. Moreover, we only tested iteration on our efficient monitor, and not on a stronger monitor, and only on coding tasks. Testing with a limited number of monitor feedback rounds, a stronger monitor to iterate against, and more general task sets would allow us to track this capability much better.

Our attacks were deliberately developed and tested in MiniLab; they do not measure how often agents would decide to coordinate on a harmful objective without being instructed to do so. They give us concrete cases for testing monitoring that follows actions and their effects across agents and services.

Future work

Building

blue-team defenses:

we think implementing synchronous monitors can partially prevent these attacks, but they remain expensive and complicated.

Oversight of the whole

: we think future monitoring measures should be developed strongly against system-wide failures or coordinated attacks. One direction is to watch the multi-agent system as a whole, reconstruct relationships among agents’ actions, and raise an alert when there is an action that looks suspicious in general, even though the monitor can’t tell whether the agents are colluding and coercing.

Investigate how

inter-agent influence

affects attack outcome and control measures (e.g., coercion): in our setup, every attacker shares the side task. A more realistic and possibly more dangerous case is a mixed team, where one misaligned agent gets honest agents to carry out parts of the attack through persuasion, misleading tickets or review comments, or coercion.
