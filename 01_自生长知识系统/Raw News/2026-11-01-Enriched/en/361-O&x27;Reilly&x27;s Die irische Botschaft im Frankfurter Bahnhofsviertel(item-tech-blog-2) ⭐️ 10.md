1. Introduction

Despite the recent successes of transformers and other sequence models, there are still many open questions about their basic principles of operation. For example, what internal representations do these models use for prediction and generation? Can we decode, interpret, and manipulate such representations? Is it even possible to consider a notion of representation without reference to an external world model?

To make progress on these questions, I propose a general way to define, decode and manipulate representations in sequence models. The approach is centered on the concept of "generalized belief states", statistics of past observations that satisfy a few criteria: (1) they are sufficient for predicting future data, (2) they can be recursively updated as new observations come in, and (3) they are low-dimensional, allowing for visualization and interpretation. I show that such generalized belief states can be mapped to the internal activations and dynamics of a sequence model, allowing for decoding and manipulation of the model's representations. I illustrate the ideas using a tractable toy model, a transformer trained on a simple HMM stochastic process, where we will see that the KV cache plays a key role.

This post is directly inspired by the

2024 paper

"Transformers Represent Belief State Geometry in their Residual Stream" by Adam Shai, Paul Riechers, and collaborators. That paper introduced the idea of studying belief states in transformers, and proposed a method for doing so using tractable toy models. I recommend reading that paper if you haven't already, though it is not a prerequisite for understanding my post. Section 5 compares this approach with the one developed in that paper.

3. Beyond belief

As discussed above, belief states formalize representations used by an optimal Bayesian predictor. Despite their attractive features, however, they face two serious limitations.

First, belief states are defined in terms of an HMM -- that is, in terms of some "ground-truth" generative model. However, in many cases, we are only given the stochastic process itself, without access to any ground-truth model. It is possible to infer an HMM for the stochastic process, but generally there is no unique solution, because the same process can be generated by many non-equivalent HMMs.

[7]

The second limitation is that, even if an HMM description is available, the resulting belief states may be unnecessarily high-dimensional. We may illustrate the problem using a simple example: suppose we first sample the hidden bias of a coin,

This limitation reflects the rigid constraints of the HMM description: the belief state is a nonnegative vector, the prediction map is linear, and the update is a normalized linear map. Such a description cannot accommodate the nonlinear prediction map in Eq. (3).

3.1 Generalized belief states

To overcome these two limitations, we introduce the concept of

generalized belief states

(GBSs). Like belief states, GBSs provide a recurrent representation of the stochastic process (i.e., they are sufficient for next-token prediction and have a recurrent update). However, GBSs differ from belief states in two ways.

First, GBSs are not defined with reference to a ground-truth generative model, such as an HMM. Instead, GBSs are chosen to be "simple", in the sense that they are low-dimensional. Formally, we seek a recurrent representation with state space

for some relatively small

Second, GBSs do not have to satisfy the rigid constraints of HMM belief states. In particular, they do not need to be normalized nonnegative vectors, and they may have nonlinear prediction and update maps. For example, for the coin process discussed above, a natural GBS is provided by the tuple

Here we have chosen to define "simplicity" in a geometric way, as low dimensionality. There is some subjectivity to this choice, and there are other ways of imposing a simplicity bias. For example, in computational mechanics, simplicity is defined as information-theoretic minimality: one seeks so-called "causal states", recurrent representations that have the smallest amount of memory about the past.

[8]

While causal states are compressed, they do not have an interpretation in terms of geometric embeddings.

It should also be said that the requirement of low dimensionality is not meaningful without constraints on the prediction and update maps. Otherwise, we can always define a trivial one-dimensional GBS representation, e.g., by encoding the entire history

into the digits of a single number and decoding it again. To prevent this trivial solution, we typically require that the prediction and update maps belong to some well-behaved class, e.g., maps that are Lipschitz continuous and have bounded image. These continuity conditions also make the geometry interpretable, in the sense that similar GBSs give rise to similar predictions and updates.

In general, a GBS is not a probability distribution, so it cannot be interpreted directly as a Bayesian posterior over latent states of some generating process. Nonetheless, if an HMM description is provided, we can find a map between GBSs and belief states that lead to the same future predictions.

[9]

For instance, in the coin example, the tuple

is not itself a probability distribution, but it can always be mapped to a probability distribution in the two-dimensional manifold of belief states

. In this sense, we may think of GBSs as low-dimensional parameterizations of belief states.

Several variants of the GBS concept have previously appeared in the literature. Linear GBSs are the states of generalized HMMs (GHMMs),

while GBSs with nonlinear maps are related to "nonlinear predictive state representations".

[11]

Also, when the stochastic process is a sequence of independent draws from a distribution with an unknown parameter, as in the coin example, a natural GBS is given by the hyperparameters of the "conjugate prior".

[12]

4. From prediction to mechanism

In the previous section, we focused on the general problem of predicting a stochastic process, without considering how the predictive model is implemented. We now use ideas developed above to study the representations used by a specific implementation, e.g., a trained transformer.

In the following, we consider a given model as an optimal predictor of the same stochastic process that the model generates. Given this process, we then find a mapping between (1) a low-dimensional GBS representation and (2) the model's high-dimensional internal activations and dynamics. This structure, which we call a

GBS abstraction

, allows us to decode and manipulate the model's beliefs in a low-dimensional space.

5. Comparison with "Transformers Represent Belief State Geometry in their Residual Stream"

As mentioned in the introduction, this post was inspired by the

2024 paper

"Transformers Represent Belief State Geometry in their Residual Stream". That paper proposed that, when a transformer is trained on data from a ground-truth HMM, the HMM's belief states will be linearly represented in the model's residual stream. To test this, one can fit a linear probe from the residual stream to the belief state and then measure its fit on held-out data.

Although the method developed in the paper seems quite promising, it has two limitations.

First, the probe target is defined as the belief state of an HMM description of the training process. However, as discussed above, we'd like to identify transformer representations without reference to a ground-truth HMM or ground-truth training data. For this reason, we propose to focus on a GBS representation of the model's intrinsic process, which can be computed purely from the model's own predictions (Sections 3 and 4.1).

Second, recall that the recurrent state of a transformer is the KV cache, not the residual stream. For this reason, it is unclear why or when the residual stream might encode a recurrent representation, such as a belief state.

[18]

In fact, in practice, belief states are not always recovered with high fidelity from the residual stream; in

my experiments

on the MRRXOR process, the held-out

for a belief state probe ranges over

–

, depending on model architecture (e.g., model size and choice of position embedding) and probe design (e.g., held-in/held-out split). Furthermore, because it does not store the recurrent state, the residual stream does not provide a natural target for causal manipulation of belief states. In essence, an intervention on the residual stream leaves the cache untouched, allowing later layers and positions to access earlier history.

To sidestep these issues, we propose to focus on the KV cache, which provides a recurrent representation of the model's stochastic process. At the same time, our analysis suggests a possible explanation for why, in practice, belief states are often recoverable from the residual stream. First, the residual stream generates the new keys and values that are written to the KV cache for later use, so it should carry more information than is needed to predict the next token. Second, since the residual stream queries the KV cache at each attention layer, the cache might "leak" a significant amount of internal information into the residual stream, especially in transformers with a high number of layers, attention heads, and embedding dimensions.

6. Summary

In this post, I proposed a way to identify, decode, and manipulate representations in autoregressive models. The approach was organized around the concepts of generalized belief states (GBSs), i.e., low-dimensional states that are recurrent and sufficient for prediction, and GBS abstractions, which allow us to connect such states to the internal activity of a statistical model. Using a small transformer trained on MRRXOR, I showed that a low-dimensional GBS abstraction can be implemented with simple decoding and encoding maps, allowing the model's beliefs to be set in the middle of a sequence.

This approach suggests the geometry of KV cache states -- possibly after mapping through attention -- as an interesting direction for further study. For example, the structure of the learned decoder and encoder, which we have not examined here, may shed light on how GBSs are encoded across cache layers, heads, and positions.

Finally, recent work has found that transformers have an inductive bias toward low-dimensional recurrent representations

[19]

; in other words, they appear to favor low-dimensional GBS representations on their own. It would be interesting to understand where this bias comes from, and whether the concepts of GBS abstractions can help explain it.
