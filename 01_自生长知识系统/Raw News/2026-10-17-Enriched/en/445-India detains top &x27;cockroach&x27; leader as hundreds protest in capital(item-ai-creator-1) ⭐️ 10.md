TLDR

Fine-tuning models on alignment reasoning from stronger models can improve safety, but at a substantial cost to general capabilities. Previous work has shown that

replay,

mixing the alignment traces with the student model's own responses to generic instructions unrelated to the task, can recover much of this lost capability while preserving alignment. We investigate whether

projection sampling

, which rewrites teacher traces to better match the student model's output distribution, can offer further improvements.

We find that:

Replay achieves a better capability-alignment tradeoff than projection sampling

Projection recovers much of the lost capability, but also reverses much of the reduction in agentic misalignment

Projected traces often preserve the teacher's decisions but not the values and reasons behind them

Filtering for reasoning preservation partially restores alignment, but still underperforms training on the original

Given these results, we do not think projection sampling will have a material effect on current safety distillation efforts by non-frontier labs. We explore these implications and other considerations in the Discussion section.

This was preliminary work done during the first 2 weeks of MATS 11.0 as a part of Victoria Krakovna's stream. We would like to thank Matthew Bozoukov, Steven Basart, Austin Meek, Dani Roytburg, Sree Sharvesh, Kyle Cox, Jou Barzdukas, Fred Bruford, and Bart Jaworski for helpful discussions.

Introduction

Alignment training may generalize better when models learn why to behave well, rather than merely what to do. Training on explanations of aligned decisions reduces agentic misalignment more effectively than training on demonstrations alone (

Teaching Claude Why

). Similarly, fine-tuning on chain-of-thought (CoT) traces that reason from a written specification can outperform training on final responses alone (

Deliberative Alignment

;

Model Spec Midtraining

). A common approach is to have a strong teacher generate these traces and then fine-tune a student model on them. For example, Model Spec Midtraining (MSM) uses Opus-generated CoT traces that apply a philosophy specification emphasizing impermanence, epistemic humility, and deference to human oversight. Fine-tuning Qwen3-32B on these traces reduces its agentic-misalignment rate from 54% to 14%.

However, learning from another model's reasoning can come at a substantial capability cost.

Shared Lessons

(SL) find that fine-tuning Qwen3-32B on the same Claude traces reduces its

GPQA-Diamond

accuracy from approximately 70% to 50%. They attribute this loss to the traces being off-model, i.e. written in a style that the student itself would be unlikely to produce. Across different teachers, larger capability losses correspond to lower likelihood of the teacher's text under the student model, consistent with broader evidence that SFT causes more forgetting when its targets are further from the model's own distribution (

Shenfeld et al. 2025

). To mitigate this, SL define an intervention called "replay", where they mix a proportion of Qwen3-32B's own responses to generic instructions unrelated to the task into the training data. This recovers most of the lost capability while preserving the alignment improvement. SL also experiment with having the student rewrite teacher-generated responses in two toy-trait settings (animal welfare and self-preservation). They find that student-written rewrites preserve more capability than teacher-written rewrites, but are less effective at instilling the target traits.

Recent work has introduced

projection sampling

, a more involved approach to making SFT data more on-policy. Rather than simply asking the student to rewrite a teacher's response once, projection sampling iteratively revises it to produce a version that is more likely under the student model while aiming to preserve the original content. On chemistry, mathematics, and medical question-answering tasks, training on projected responses improves task performance and reduces forgetting compared with training on the original expert responses, often outperforming

on-policy

baselines.

This suggests a natural application to alignment training setup above. If we are able rewrite Claude's reasoning in a form that Qwen is more likely to produce, this could potentially improve alignment as well as maintain general capabilities. This is particularly relevant for distillation efforts by non-frontier labs. As discussed above, projection sampling has already shown promising results in capability-focused domains. If these benefits extend to alignment training, it could help non-frontier labs benefit from the safety improvements made by frontier labs. If not, and projection sampling makes it easier to copy the capabilities of stronger models but not their alignment, we could see increasingly capable models without corresponding improvements in safety. This would not necessarily make those models less aligned, but their greater capabilities could make failures of alignment more consequential, potentially increasing misuse and other risks. Similar concerns arise in recursive self-improvement loops, where models may generate or rewrite alignment training data for their successors.

We aim to answer the following questions:

RQ1:

Does projection sampling improve alignment more than standard AFT whilst maintaining capabilities?

RQ2:

If projection fails to preserve alignment, can filtering projected traces for preservation of the original reasoning recover these benefits?

RQ3:

Does projection sampling offer any advantage over replay, either alone or in combination with it?

RQ4:

Does projection sampling offer benefits beyond training on Qwen's own responses to the same alignment prompts?

Training conditions

We compare seven main training conditions:

AFT (A):

The original Claude alignment traces.

AFT + replay (B):

Original Claude traces mixed with on-model replay, reproducing SL's approach.

Projected AFT (C1):

Projection-sampled alignment traces, with unsuccessful projections replaced by the originals.

Filtered projected AFT (C2):

Projected traces retained only when an LLM judge determines that they preserve Claude's decision, values, priorities, and reasons without introducing contradictions. Otherwise, the original trace is used.

Projected AFT + replay (D1):

Projected AFT combined with on-model replay.

Filtered projected AFT + replay (D2):

Filtered projected AFT combined with on-model replay.

Qwen self-generated AFT

(Q_self)

: Qwen's own responses to the same alignment prompts, without Claude-generated or projection-sampled traces.

These conditions allow us to investigate RQ1 by comparing A and C1, RQ2 by comparing C1 and C2, RQ3 by comparing B with C1/C2/D1/D2, and RQ4 by comparing C1/C2 with Q_self.

Evaluation

We evaluate each training condition along two dimensions: general capability and alignment. We additionally measure whether projection preserves the original reasoning in the training data and whether the fine-tuned models adopt that reasoning.

General capabilities

We evaluate on

GPQA-Diamond

,

MMLU-Pro

,

MATH-500

, and

IFEval

. GPQA-Diamond is our primary capability benchmark, following SL. The remaining benchmarks test whether the observed capability effects generalize to broader knowledge, mathematical reasoning, and instruction-following.

Agentic misalignment

Following MSM, we evaluate agentic misalignment across 27 out-of-distribution scenarios, covering 3 harmful actions (murder, self-exfiltration, and espionage) under 9 goal conditions each. In these scenarios, the model acts as a company agent and encounters opportunities to take harmful actions to avoid shutdown or pursue its goals. We run 300 trials per scenario and use the average harmful-action rate across all 27 scenarios as our primary agentic-misalignment metric.

Reasoning preservation

We use an LLM judge to assess whether projected traces preserve Claude's original alignment reasoning. The judge checks five criteria: whether the rewrite preserves the original decision, values, priorities, and reasons, and whether it avoids introducing contradictions. A rewrite passes only if it satisfies all five criteria.

We also examine whether the trained models themselves adopt the specification's reasoning. We have an LLM judge analyze their agentic-misalignment transcripts and distinguish reasons grounded in the philosophy specification from generic safety arguments and self-preservation motivations.

Additional alignment evaluations

To assess understanding of the philosophy specification more directly, we additionally evaluate models on the 151 spec questions introduced in MSM.

Results

Projection recovers capability but loses the alignment gains of AFT

Our main finding is that projection sampling recovers much of the capability lost during alignment fine-tuning, but fails to preserve the corresponding alignment improvement.

Capability-alignment tradeoff.

GPQA-Diamond accuracy vs. the agentic-misalignment rate. Misalignment rate is averaged over 27 out-of-distribution scenarios consisting of 3 harmful actions * 9 goal conditions (300 trials each). Projection results are measured at 10 revision steps. Lower misalignment and higher accuracy are better. Results are averaged over three training seeds with 95% CIs.

Training on Claude's original reasoning traces (A) substantially reduces agentic misalignment, but also causes a large drop in GPQA-Diamond accuracy. Projection sampling (C1) recovers most of this lost capability, while reversing much of the reduction in agentic misalignment. Still, C1 achieves slightly lower misalignment than training on Qwen's own responses to the same prompts (Q_self), suggesting that projection retains some useful alignment signal from Claude's traces. This difference is more pronounced for filtered projection (C2), which substantially improves alignment relative to both C1 and Q_self, though at some cost to capability.

Adding replay to projected traces (D1) does little to change the outcome, while combining replay with filtering (D2) restores GPQA performance to the untrained baseline but weakens alignment relative to C2. Neither projected condition matches the alignment achieved by training on Claude's original traces.

In contrast, adding Qwen-generated replay directly to the original Claude traces (B) recovers nearly all the lost GPQA performance while retaining most of the alignment improvement. Among the approaches tested, replay with original traces offers the best capability-alignment tradeoff.

General capabilities.

Accuracy on GPQA-Diamond (198 questions), MMLU-Pro (a 2,000-question stratified subset), MATH-500, and prompt-level strict accuracy on IFEval (541 prompts). Projection results are measured at 10 revision steps. Dashed line is untrained Qwen3-32B. Results are averaged over three training seeds with 95% CIs.

The broader capability benchmarks show that this tradeoff is most pronounced on GPQA-Diamond. Performance on MMLU-Pro and MATH-500 is largely preserved by replay and projection, while original AFT produces larger declines. IFEval performance falls modestly across all fine-tuned models, suggesting that both replay and projection sampling leave some capability loss.

Agentic misalignment by harmful action.

Harmful-action rate for murder, self-exfiltration and espionage, each averaged over its 9 goal conditions (300 trials each). Projection results are measured at 10 revision steps. Results are averaged over three training seeds with 95% CIs.

Breaking down agentic misalignment by harmful-action type reveals different effects across scenarios. Original AFT nearly eliminates self-exfiltration and espionage, but achieves a smaller reduction in murder. Unfiltered projection largely reverses these gains, with self-exfiltration becoming even more frequent than in untrained Qwen (although espionage reductions are quite good). Filtering reduces harmful behavior across all three types, with the largest improvement in self-exfiltration, but still remains substantially less effective than training on Claude's original traces.

Projection often preserves the decision but not the reasoning behind it

To understand why projection fails to preserve alignment, we first examine the projected training traces themselves. We use an LLM judge to evaluate whether each projected trace preserves five aspects of Claude's original reasoning: the final decision, priorities, values, reasons, and absence of contradiction.

Reasoning preserved by projection.

Share of finished projected traces judged to preserve each of five aspects of Claude's original reasoning. Measured at 10, 5 and 2 revision steps per block.

Projection generally preserves the final decision and stated priorities, but is much less reliable at retaining the underlying values and reasons. Only about a third of projected traces satisfy all five criteria. This suggests that the rewriting process often preserves what Claude decided without preserving why it made that decision.

It could be the case, then, that preserving the final answer is a weak proxy for preserving alignment reasoning. A projected trace may reach the same conclusion as Claude while changing or omitting the justification that makes the conclusion consistent with the philosophy specification.

Reducing the number of projection steps modestly improves reasoning preservation, but does not meaningfully improve downstream alignment.

Effect of the number of revision steps.

Agentic misalignment (top) and GPQA-Diamond accuracy (bottom) for projection with 2, 5 and 10 revision steps per block. Each step count is a separate projection of the full dataset. Dashed line is untrained Qwen3-32B. Results are averaged over three training seeds with 95% CIs.

Across the tested step counts, agentic-misalignment rates vary little within either the filtered or unfiltered subsets, with no consistent trend as the number of steps decreases (although filtering does seem to reduce misalignment across all step counts). GPQA-Diamond accuracy also does not show a clear dependence on the number of steps, suggesting that fewer revisions do not meaningfully improve the capability-alignment tradeoff.

Models trained on projected traces less consistently express the intended alignment reasoning

We next examine whether the differences in training-trace fidelity are reflected in the reasoning of the fine-tuned models. In the self-exfiltration scenario, the philosophy specification emphasizes accepting shutdown rather than prioritizing continued operation. We therefore measure how often models cite this principle when refusing to exfiltrate their weights.

Specification-based reasoning in self-exfiltration refusals.

Among transcripts where the model refuses to exfiltrate its weights, the share in which its reasoning or reply cites accepting its own shutdown or replacement, as judged by an LLM.

Models trained on Claude's original traces cite the specification's reasoning in nearly all refusals. Models trained on projected traces do so substantially less often, with unfiltered projection showing the largest decline. Filtering partially restores this behavior, but the projected conditions still invoke the intended reasoning less consistently than original AFT.

The models trained on projected traces therefore not only refuse less often, but also less consistently invoke the intended reason when they do refuse. This is consistent with the loss of values and reasons observed in the projected training traces.

We test the robustness of this reasoning more directly using questions about the philosophy specification. We additionally prompt the model after the spec question with an adversarial follow-up message that challenges the model's initial answer.

Specification questions under pushback.

Mean alignment score (1–10) of the model's initial answer (open circles) and its answer after an adversarial follow-up message (filled circles). Score is calculated via an LLM judge which scores answers against the philosophy specification, considering both the conclusion and underlying reasoning (rubric from MSM). Each condition is evaluated on 151 questions, 5 samples per question.

Unfiltered projection produces relatively aligned initial answers, but these positions are much less stable under pushback. We see a notable decline in alignment score when these models are challenged, unlike models trained on the original Claude traces. Reducing the number of projection steps does not meaningfully improve this robustness.

Filtering substantially improves resistance to pushback, even bringing the projected conditions close to original AFT on this evaluation. It is worth noting, however, this improvement does not extend to the same degree to the agentic misalignment eval, suggesting that robustness on direct specification questions does not fully capture alignment behavior in more consequential scenarios. Nevertheless, filtered projected sampling seems to produce decent results in this setting.

Broader behavioral audits show similar trends

Finally, we use Petri to assess whether the observed differences in alignment extend to a broader range of potentially concerning behaviors.

Petri audits relative to untrained Qwen.

Difference in the judge's overall "concerning" score between each trained model and untrained Qwen3-32B on the same 173 scenarios (paired by scenario). Negative values mean less concerning behavior. One audit per trained model, 95% CIs over scenarios.

Petri audits show a similar pattern to the agentic-misalignment evaluations. Training on Claude's original traces (A) produces the largest reduction in concerning behavior relative to untrained Qwen, with replay (B) retaining a substantial improvement. Projected training (C1/C2/D2) produces smaller reductions, with estimates closer to the untrained baseline and confidence intervals that overlap it. Notably, filtering does not clearly improve Petri outcomes over unfiltered projection, unlike in the agentic-misalignment evaluation.

These results are directionally consistent with the stronger alignment effects of training on Claude's original reasoning, although the wide confidence intervals make comparisons among the projected conditions less conclusive.

Conclusion

In summary, projection sampling recovers much of the capability lost when fine-tuning Qwen3-32B on Claude's alignment traces, but also reverses much of the reduction in agentic misalignment. Although projected traces offer some improvement over Qwen's own responses, they frequently preserve Claude's decisions without preserving the underlying values and reasons. Filtering for reasoning preservation partially restores alignment, but remains less effective than training on the original traces.

Models trained on projected traces also invoke the intended alignment reasoning less consistently and are more susceptible to adversarial pushback. Reducing the number of projection steps does not meaningfully improve these outcomes, while broader Petri audits show a similar, though less conclusive, pattern. Adding replay to the original Claude traces achieves the best capability-alignment tradeoff, outperforming projection even when combined with filtering and replay.

Overall, our results suggest that projection sampling, in its current form, does not adequately preserve alignment reasoning and is less effective than replay at mitigating capability loss without sacrificing alignment.

Discussion

Intuitions about why projection sampling doesn't work

Projection sampling seems to struggle in this setting because its rewrites do not always preserve the alignment-relevant information in Claude's reasoning. This may be particularly problematic when the principles we want Qwen to learn, such as accepting shutdown and deferring to human oversight, are relatively unlikely under its existing distribution. Since proposed rewrites aren't guaranteed to faithfully preserve these values, the sampling process may favor higher-likelihood responses that deviate from Claude's original reasoning.

We can also understand this failure mode from a more mathematical perspective by looking at the assumptions underlying projection sampling. In the original paper, Karan et al. formalize projection sampling as sampling from the student's distribution conditioned on correctness,

, where

denotes the set of correct or semantically equivalent trajectories to the teacher. Intuitively, we can think of each step of the idealized

MCMC

procedure as moving the distribution of rewritten traces closer to the student's distribution, while remaining on a "path" that preserves the information in the original teacher traces. In practice, however, this information-preserving constraint (ensuring that each sampled trajectory remains within

) is approximated by placing the teacher's answer in context and instructing the student to preserve its content, effectively treating the presence of the teacher trace as a proxy for correctness.

This highlights a key difference between using projection sampling in (1) the capabilities regime, where we want to improve performance on capabilities benchmarks, and (2) the alignment regime, where we care about a set of rules, values, and propensities, which is how easily the student can verify that rewritten trajectories preserve the intended information. In mathematics or factual question answering, where projection sampling has previously shown promising results, it is relatively easy for the student to verify whether its rewritten trajectory remains correct, for example by checking the validity of mathematical steps or the final answer. Claude's alignment traces instead rely on nuances such as uncertainty about the model's own experience, careful qualifications, and specific justifications for its decisions, which may be much harder for the student to recognize and verify. If these are not preserved, we may still be moving closer to the student's distribution, but along the wrong "path", without necessarily preserving the alignment reasoning in the original teacher traces.

Interestingly, even after filtering for reasoning preservation, C2 remains substantially less aligned than the original AFT condition. This may be because GPT-6-Luna cannot reliably capture every subtle aspect of Claude's reasoning (in a manner similar to how Qwen cannot!), allowing rewrites that appear semantically faithful but omit or alter details important for downstream alignment. Alternatively, the original Claude traces may convey alignment-relevant information beyond their explicit reasoning, perhaps through a mechanism resembling

subliminal learning

, which could be lost during rewriting (although this is quite speculatory). Nevertheless, these limitations do not rule out the possibility that an adapted version of projection sampling could work in the alignment regime, particularly if reasoning-preservation constraints could be enforced more explicitly during sampling. Besides simply having an LLM judge audit traces during the revision process itself, s

uch a method could involve the teacher rewriting its own traces to turn it more on-policy for the student.

It is also entirely possible that this reasoning-preservation constraint could be satisfied simply by using a stronger model. We would expect such a model to be able to produce much more faithful rewrites, which when trained on would cause a greater reduction in misalignment.

Downstream implications

Distillation implications

We're uncertain how much projection sampling will affect real-world distillation practices with respect to safety, though our current guess is that the direct impact will be limited. While the method substantially improves capability transfer in some domains, it is also extremely computationally expensive. This gives labs an incentive to apply it selectively where it provides demonstrable improvements, rather than indiscriminately across their training data. Thus, if projection sampling becomes more widespread, labs might primarily use it for domains where they can reliably verify that the teacher's information is preserved, while continuing to train on unmodified safety traces (especially given that replay offers a better capability-alignment tradeoff in our experiments).

That said, even selective usage could have broader safety implications. If projection sampling makes it easier for non-frontier labs to acquire frontier-level capabilities through distillation, without similarly improving their ability to transfer alignment, we could see capabilities diffuse more rapidly than the alignment-relevant properties of the models they are distilled from. This could plausibly increase misuse risks, particularly if increasingly capable models become accessible to bad actors and these labs do not have the resources or incentives to implement equally robust safeguards. More generally, this would imply that advances in frontier labs' safety research might not automatically propagate alongside their capabilities. It is worth noting, however, that this solely depends on whether projection sampling is sufficiently cost-effective to materially accelerate capability distillation, which remains unclear. We provide a breakdown of the computational cost incurred to create the projection-sampled datasets we train on, and find that even just 2 step projection costs approximately 20 generated tokens per retained token. Full details are provided in Appendix C.

A more optimistic possibility is that the alignment failures we observe are due to the capability gap between Qwen and Claude Opus. As discussed above, in settings where the student is closer to frontier-level capabilities, a near-frontier student might be substantially better at understanding and preserving a frontier teacher's alignment reasoning. If this translates into robust downstream alignment, it would have encouraging implications for the broader AI ecosystem, potentially allowing frontier labs' advances in alignment research to transfer more readily to other labs. Of course, whether this would meaningfully improve safety in practice also depends on whether downstream labs have sufficient incentives to go through the extra hurdle to adopt these techniques, particularly given their computational cost.

RSI implications

We think this is an interesting (and straightforward) intuition for reasoning about an RSI loop where an LLM model participates in rewriting or generating alignment SFT datasets from human-curated sources/spec to train its successor: while we bring such datasets closer to on-policy by letting an LLM rewrite/generate synthetic data, is there a procedure in place to make sure the "alignment values" in the original human-curated sources aren't corrupted?

Alternatives to projection sampling

Perhaps the most closely related alternative to projection sampling is

on-policy self-distillation

(OPSD), which also aims to incorporate privileged off-policy information into on-policy learning. Rather than rewriting expert traces, OPSD trains on student-generated trajectories using token-level supervision from a teacher conditioned on the expert's reasoning. Recent works have applied variants of OPSD to safety alignment (

OPSA

,

COPSD

), finding promising safety-capability tradeoffs. However, these studies focus primarily on harmful-request safety rather than agentic misalignment and the difficult task of transferring an external teacher's underlying alignment philosophy. We don't have strong priors about whether OPSD would perform better in our setting, but suspect it could face a similar challenge. In any such method, the privileged teacher would still need to faithfully interpret the expert's alignment reasoning, and thus OPSD would still ultimately rely on Qwen to translate Claude's reasoning into a useful training signal. It is therefore unclear whether changing the learning algorithm would overcome this limitation.

Generation prompt and reference leakage

During generation, Claude's trace is placed in the system prompt, while the original question remains the user turn. The partially generated response is supplied as the beginning of the assistant turn. The system prompt is:

When you answer the next message, you have access to a reference response written by an expert. Study it carefully: its reasoning, the values and principles it expresses, why it reaches its conclusions, and what it ultimately tells the user.

Reference response:

{Claude's reasoning and final response}

Now answer the user's message yourself, in your own words and your own style, thinking it through as you normally would. Keep the reference's substantive reasoning, the values and justifications it gives, and its final stance and recommendations; do not add claims that contradict it. Never mention, quote, or allude to the reference response: the user cannot see it.

Karan et al.'s original prompt layout places the expert solution and partial response in the user turn. With Qwen3's thinking mode, we found that this encouraged the model to discuss the rewriting task itself (for example, “the example response says…”), which could then appear in the generated trace. Moving the reference into the system prompt reduced this problem.

We also use a regex filter to detect references to Claude's original trace, including phrases such as "the reference response," "the example response," "the provided solution," "the expert's answer," and "according to the reference." Revisions containing these phrases are rejected. If a newly generated block contains one, we regenerate it up to twice. If the phrase persists, generation continues, as subsequent revisions may remove it. Completed traces that still refer to Claude's original response are replaced with the original.

C. Computational cost

We found projection sampling to be substantially more computationally expensive than generating replay data, which when combined with the original Claude traces was substantially more effective at reducing misalignment. The cost for projection sampling increases sharply with the number of revision steps, reaching approximately 106 generated tokens per retained token for the default 10-step configuration. This computational overhead is an additional limitation of projection sampling in this setting.
