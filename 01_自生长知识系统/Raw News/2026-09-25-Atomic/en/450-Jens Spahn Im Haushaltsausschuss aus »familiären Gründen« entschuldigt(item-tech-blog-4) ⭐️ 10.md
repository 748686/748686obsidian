### [Higher Elo recruits deeper attention layers in frozen Maia-3 chess transformer](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/HzeuyQvqN4nHHvaXc/increasing-skill-level-recruits-deeper-attention-layers-in-a) ⭐️ ?/10

David Litman&\#x27;s analysis of Maia-3, a frozen 8-layer transformer that conditions on Elo, shows that raising the skill dial from 700 to 2500 shifts computation deeper across all measured chess pieces and move types. The effect is measured by causal center of mass derived from attention-head ablations and is strongest for specific tactics such as knight forks. Rather than moving earlier for reuse, later heads are increasingly recruited for specialized computations while earlier heads contribute roughly consistently. The monotonic depth migration was statistically significant \(p < 0.001 in permutation tests against shuffled Elo labels\) across the fork-control subset.

rss · LessWrong AI风险社区 · Sep 24, 20:30

**「Background」** Maia models are chess transformers trained to imitate human players at different Elo levels, and Maia-3 can vary its output skill without weight changes by conditioning on an Elo input. The analysis uses attention-head ablation to quantify each head&\#x27;s causal influence on move logits and summarizes layer usage with a mass-weighted average depth, called causal center of mass.

**「Impact」** The findings challenge the expectation that higher skill pushes feature reuse earlier in the network and instead suggest deeper reasoning depends on recruiting later transformer layers. This points to a concrete, testable mechanism for how conditional skill scaling can change internal computation in frozen language-style architectures applied to deterministic domains like chess.

**「Community Discussion」** No community comments were available for this item. Litman notes that Tom Griffiths initially found a shallower-migration prediction plausible before the data showed the opposite pattern.

### [State of Pandemic Early Warning: Biosecurity Memo Analysis](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/uYd2ZdFMLyGPhLtqY/state-of-pandemic-early-warning) ⭐️ ?/10

In a memo presented at the Summer 2026 Biosecurity Summit, Jeff Tkachuk outlines a framework for biosurveillance designed to deter and detect biological attacks, emphasizing the need for systems robust against adversarial manipulation. The analysis identifies three critical applications: detecting stealth pandemics \(pathogens with delayed or unremarkable symptoms\), triggering timely initial responses to outbreaks, and enabling ongoing suppression through monitoring. Key historical comparisons include the overreaction to 1976 swine flu, the underreaction to 2014 and 2026 Ebola outbreaks, and the effective response to 2009 H1N1, highlighting how current COVID-weary public health climates may lead to dangerous delays. The author argues that while deterrence-by-denial requires both effective detection and public confidence in it, deterrence-by-punishment is less reliable against non-state or suicidal actors.

rss · LessWrong AI风险社区 · Sep 24, 18:10

**「Background」** Biosurveillance refers to the systematic monitoring of biological threats through data collection, sequencing, and analysis to detect outbreaks early. Stealth pandemics represent a category of biothreat where pathogens evade standard detection due to mild or absent initial symptoms, potentially allowing widespread transmission before serious effects manifest. The 2026 Ebola outbreak mentioned reflects recent real-world events where diagnostic limitations \(such as PCR tests failing to detect certain strains\) contributed to response delays.

**「Impact」** The memo suggests that current biosurveillance gaps leave civilizations vulnerable to deliberate biological attacks that exploit diagnostic blind spots or delay response protocols. Organizations investing in pandemic preparedness should prioritize ambiguous early-detection systems over high-sensitivity but narrow-scope approaches to reduce attacker options.
