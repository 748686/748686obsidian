The articles were thematically linked under the Cultural Heritage section but addressed separate subjects:

## Core Facts

### A. Konglong Village Site (孔龙村遗址), Sichuan

- **Location**: Konglong Village, Marlborough City (马尔康市), Aba Tibetan and Qiang Autonomous Prefecture, Sichuan Province. Situated on a second terrace of the Jiaomuz River (脚木足河), the main source of the Dadu River, at ~2,500 m altitude.
- **Age**: ~5,500–4,800 years BP.
- **Scale**: Over 100,000 m²; 17,000 m² excavated since June 2023.
- **Significance**: The earliest, largest, and most artifact-rich Neolithic site yet discovered in western Sichuan. A complete prehistoric central settlement with residential areas, public buildings, craft production zones, a large moat, and a cemetery.
- **Painted pottery**: Nearly 10,000 fragments recovered, mostly from domestic refuse deposits. Over 10完整 vessels and 20+完整 decorative patterns reconstructed.
- **Cultural attribution**:
  - Early phase: Typical Late Yangshao (Shilingxia type / 石岭下类型) style — first concentrated discovery of this type in western Sichuan plateau.
  - Middle–late phase: Mix of Majiayao type styles and local characteristics.
- **Transmission stages identified**:
  1. **Initiation phase** (c. 5,500–5,100 BP): Yangshao Late / Shilingxia type. Similar pottery found at Dadiwan, Fujiamen, Shan纳shuzha, Dali Jiaping in Gansu.
  2. **Prosperity phase** (c. 5,100–4,900 BP): Majiayao Early–Middle. Found across Dadu River upper reaches and Min River upper reaches (Haxiu, Liuji zhai, Yingpanshan, Jiangweicheng, Konglongcun, Han'eyi).
  3. **Decline phase** (c. 4,900–4,700 BP): Majiayao Late. Wider distribution but declining quality and quantity.
- **Production mode**: Early painted pottery likely directly imported via migration or trade; later phases show increasing local production with different clay compositions and some local motifs.
- **Broader cultural links**: Shared elements between western Sichuan and Yellow River upper reaches include millet/millet dryland agriculture, pottery kilns, house foundations, hearths, adornments (bangles, hairpins), bird/frog totems, ritual objects (pottery drums, bells, stone chimes, jade axes).
- **Author**: Gao Han (高寒), Sichuan Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology.

### B. "Qin People Valued Martial Prowess" Re-evaluation

- **Thesis**: The stereotype "Qin people were uniquely martial" (秦人尚武) is a misreading that ignores the universal warring-states context and Qin's own civil-literary dimensions.
- **Evidence of pan-Zhanguo martial culture**:
  - States with documented martial traditions: Qi (勇爵, 技击), Wei (武卒 selection), Zhao (胡服骑射), Chu (筚路蓝缕), Han (劲韩, bows/crossbows), Yan (Yue Yi's five-state coalition).
  - *Classic of Poetry* (*Shijing*): 30+ poems on warfare across states, not exclusive to Qin.
- **Archaeological evidence of multi-state military development**:
  - **Chu**: 500+ weapons from 558 tombs at Yutaishan; double-bolt repeating crossbow at Qinjiazui; 32 bronze swords at Tianxingguan No. 1 (some with tin-enrichment hardening).
  - **Yan**: Iron weapons (swords, spears, halberds, helmets) from Yanshan No. 44 warrior mass grave; high-carbon steel quenching technique dated to Late Warring States.
  - **Zeng**: 4,700+ weapons from Marquis Yi of Zeng tomb; refined lamellar armor representing peak of pre-Qin armor craft.
  - **San Jin / Han**: "令工师冶" three-tier supervision system; 180+ bronze ge/spears from Zheng-Han ancient city cache with dated inscriptions and stone molds.
- **Sources of the "Qin martial" stereotype**:
  1. Warring States纵横家 (strategists) using enemy-rhetoric for diplomatic purposes.
  2. Retrospective bias: interpreting Qin's unification success as evidence of inherent ethnic character rather than institutional achievement.
- **Counter-evidence of Qin's civil dimension**:
  - *Shuihudi Qin bamboo texts* ("为吏之道"): officials required integrity, uprightness, prudence — Confucian moral concepts.
  - Private seals from Fengxiang Yongcheng and Xianyang: inscriptions like "忠仁思士" expressing loyalty, benevolence, reverence.
  - "敬事" (respectful diligence) stamp on pottery pads at Xianyang workshop site — from *Analects*.
  - Terracotta army senior officer figurines: restrained posture, sword at side, emphasizing strategy over brute aggression.
  - Civil-military overlap: Neisi Teng governed territories and led campaigns; Gan Mao drafted land laws and conquered Yi yang.
  - **Naming statistics**: Among 680 decipherable Qin bamboo names, virtue-class names total 64 — most common expressing humility, loyalty, benevolence; "bravery" names were rarest. Reflects popular values diverging from state meritocratic rewards for military achievement.
- **Author**: Xu Weihong (许卫红), Shaanxi Provincial Institute of Archaeology.

### C. Bao Zheng Stele Discovery, Kaifeng

- **Context**: Author Han Yang grew up in Kaifeng; visited Bao Gong Shrine near maternal grandmother's home in childhood.
- **Previously known inscription**: "Record of Inspectors of Kaifeng Prefecture" (*开封府题名记*) stele in Kaifeng Museum lists former prefects including Fan Zhongyan and Ouyang Xiu; Bao Zheng's name is absent — reportedly worn away by centuries of visitors rubbing it, creating the folk saying "碑上无名，但有口皆碑" (nameless on stele, yet eulogized by all).
- **New discovery (2023)**:
  - Author saw a rubbings image at a cultural relics museum inside Qingming Riverside Landscape Garden: seal-script inscription "龙马负图处" (Where the Dragon-Horse Bore the Diagram), signed "Jiayou Year 2, 3rd month, Longtuge Academic, Prefect of Kaifeng, Bao Zheng" (嘉祐二年三月龙图阁学士知开封府包拯).
  - Father learned from a friend (北大寺 administrator) that the original stele exists at **Beida Temple (北大寺)**, near Henan University.
  - Stele is mounted on the northern archway wall of the main hall's卷棚.
  - Opposite (south wall) is a Qing-dynasty stele by minister **A'gui (阿桂)** titled "Record of the Bao Zheng Stele" (*包拯碑铭记*), explaining:
    - Discovered in 1785 (Qianlong 50) by Henan provincial intendant **Jiang Lan (江兰)** after a dream.
    - A'gui wrote: "汴梁为宋故都，孝肃为宋名臣，大书深刻，信而有征."
    - Scholars suggest the stele originally stood at Cangwang Temple; destroyed by Yellow River flood in late Ming, later rediscovered.
  - In late Qing (Guangxu era), during Beida Temple reconstruction, the temple abbot — a collector of epigraphic material — relocated both steles to the temple.
- **Current status**: Protected as cultural relic; responsibility plaques installed in 2023.
- **Author**: Han Yang (韩阳).
