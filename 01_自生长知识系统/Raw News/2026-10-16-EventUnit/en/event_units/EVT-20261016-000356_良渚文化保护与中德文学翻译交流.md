# Event: Cultural Heritage Preservation (Liangzhu) and Sino-German Literary Translation Exchange (Ma Hai Mo)

## Event Overview

On October 16, 2026, *People’s Daily* published two distinct articles reporting on cultural activities in Hangzhou, China, and Bonn, Germany. The first article (Article #537) is an advertisement (Page 16) focusing on the protection of the Liangzhu ancient city site in Yuhang District, Hangzhou, framing it as a contribution to "civilizational mutual learning." The second article (Article #538, Page 17) is an interview with German translator Ma Hai Mo, detailing his career translating Chinese literature into German and his philosophy of cross-cultural exchange. While both articles touch upon themes of cultural heritage and exchange, they originate from different sections of the newspaper (Advertisement vs. International Supplement) and focus on different geographical and thematic domains (Chinese archaeological heritage preservation vs. Sino-German literary translation).

**2. Ma Hai Mo’s Literary Translation Career (Source: Article #538)**
*   **Subject:** Ma Hai Mo (马海默), a German translator and scholar based in Bonn.
*   **Role:** Recognized as one of the most important German translators of contemporary Chinese literature.
*   **Key Translations:**
    *   Writers: Alai (阿来), Bi Feiyu (毕飞宇), Su Tong (苏童), Liu Cixin (刘慈欣), Chen Qiufan (陈楸帆).
    *   Specific Works: Bi Feiyu’s *Qingyi* (《青衣》), Liu Cixin’s works, Chen Qiufan’s *Wastelander* (《荒潮》), and over 30 Chinese graphic novels/comics including *Bu Tian Ge* (《步天歌》), *Dian Jiao Zhang Wang* (《踮脚张望》), and *Yin Shan Meng Tan* (《隐山梦谈》).
*   **Professional Background:**
    *   Studied sinology at the University of Bonn.
    *   Doctoral mentor: Wolfgang Kubin (沃尔夫冈·顾彬), a German sinologist and translator who received the Chinese Government Friendship Award.
    *   First Chinese translation: Yu Dafu’s *Sinking* (《沉沦》), translated in 2002 at Kubin’s request for his *History of Chinese Literature*.
    *   First published translation: Bi Feiyu’s *Qingyi*, published four years later (approx. 2006) under Kubin’s recommendation.
*   **Translation Philosophy:**
    *   Self-identifies as a "traveler of the soul" (心灵的漫游者), emphasizing imagination and internal exploration over factual reporting.
    *   Opposes literal fidelity; advocates for capturing the spirit, imagery, and aesthetic experience of the original text.
    *   Views translation as a creative intellectual labor and a dialogue where the "I" is constructed through encounter with the "You" (China/the other).
    *   Notes that Chinese and German syntax differ significantly; for example, the phrase "I love you" has multiple sentence structures in German, requiring adaptation to avoid monotony.
*   **Literary Preferences:**
    *   Favorite German author: Franz Kafka (recently revisited *The Trial* and *The Castle*).
    *   Interests: Contemporary Chinese novels, comics, and science fiction.
    *   Disagreement with mentor: Kubin believes storytelling novels are outdated; Ma Hai Mo argues storytelling is a human instinct essential for understanding the world.
    *   Influences: Laozi’s *Tao Te Ching* (considered the most influential book in his life) and Zhuangzi (target for future translation).

## Different Country / Regional Perspectives

*   **Chinese Perspective (Domestic):** The Liangzhu article presents a narrative of national heritage preservation and soft power projection ("civilizational mutual learning"). The Ma Hai Mo article highlights the successful internationalization of Chinese contemporary literature and the deep personal connections Chinese writers build with foreign translators. It positions China as a source of cultural richness (ancient philosophy, modern sci-fi, comics) engaging the world.
*   **German Perspective (International):** Ma Hai Mo’s interview provides an insider’s view from Germany. He notes that the German publishing market has limited space for foreign translated works, making cultural soft power crucial for reach. He advocates for breaking stereotypes of China through diverse, contemporary youth literature rather than just traditional or political narratives. He emphasizes the role of translators as "actors" who must adapt language (e.g., 20+ words for "money" in German) to serve the reader’s experience, reflecting a German scholarly tradition of deep textual engagement (Bonn Sinology).

## Information Differences and Conflicts

*   **Conflict on Storytelling:** A notable difference exists between Ma Hai Mo and his mentor Wolfgang Kubin. Kubin believes that "storytelling novels are outdated today," whereas Ma Hai Mo argues that "storytelling is human nature" and that people always use stories to understand themselves and the world. Ma Hai Mo explicitly identifies as non-"elitist" in this regard, contrasting with his mentor’s perceived stance.
*   **Genre Divergence:** While Kubin focused on classical Chinese poetry, Ma Hai Mo deliberately chose contemporary novels, science fiction, and comics. This represents a shift from traditional literary forms to popular and contemporary genres in the translation of Chinese culture.
