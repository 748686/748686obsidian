## Core Facts
*   **Publication Details:** The article was published in *People's Daily* on September 15, 2026, on Page 10 (Society Section).
*   **Expert Attribution:** Dr. Liu Zunjin, Director of Neurology at Peking University People’s Hospital and Chair of the Department of Neurology at Peking University, provided clinical insights.
*   **Triggering Context:** The article notes that the recent death of a prominent host due to acute cerebral hemorrhage has brought attention to the disease, correcting the misconception that it is exclusively an "elderly disease."
*   **Medical Definition:** Cerebral hemorrhage (commonly known as cerebral apoplexy or hemorrhagic stroke) is a severe type of stroke caused by ruptured intracranial vessels, leading to blood leakage that compresses and damages brain tissue. It is characterized by rapid onset and a very short rescue window.
*   **Clinical Classifications:**
    1.  **Intraparenchymal Hemorrhage:** Most common, often in the basal ganglia. Primary cause is hypertension (poorly controlled or fluctuating). Other risks include smoking, abnormal blood glucose, atherosclerosis, and congenital vascular malformations.
    2.  **Subarachnoid Hemorrhage:** Occurs on the brain's surface, primarily caused by ruptured intracranial aneurysms, characterized by intense pain.
*   **High-Risk Groups Identified:**
    *   Individuals with poor blood pressure control (primary risk group).
    *   Individuals with congenital vascular abnormalities (e.g., aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, moyamoya disease).
    *   Patients with underlying conditions (diabetes, atherosclerosis, blood disorders) or those on long-term anticoagulants/antiplatelets (Warfarin, Aspirin, Clopidogrel).
    *   Individuals with a family history of hypertension.
    *   Young/middle-aged adults with poor lifestyle habits (chronic sleep deprivation, high stress, irregular routines, substance abuse).
*   **Warning Signs:** Sudden abnormal headache, projectile vomiting, lethargy, limb weakness, slurred speech, and blurred vision.
*   **Emergency Protocol:** Call 120 immediately. Do not self-medicate or move the patient. Keep the patient flat, head turned to one side to maintain airway. Record the time of onset. Prioritize professional transport unless distance is very short.
